Aim

One project that ties the data link layer together

Textbooks teach framing, error detection and flow control as separate topics, and lab programs usually implement one at a time. LinkLab joins them into a single working pipeline, the way a real link uses them. A message gets a sequence number and a CRC. It is bit-stuffed, corrupted by a simulated noisy channel, checked by the receiver and retransmitted until it arrives intact.

The project then goes one step further than a demo. In the Experiments tab it runs thousands of randomized trials and reports how often each detection method catches each kind of error, and how Stop-and-Wait, Go-Back-N and Selective Repeat compare as the link gets worse.

Viva preparation

Questions you are likely to get

Why is the flag 01111110, and why stuff after five 1s?

The flag is the only pattern with six 1s in a row. Inserting a 0 after any five consecutive 1s in the data guarantees six 1s never appear inside a frame, so the receiver can always find frame boundaries.

How does CRC detect errors?

The sender appends r zeros to the data, divides by the generator using modulo-2 (XOR) division, and sends the data plus the r-bit remainder. The whole codeword is then exactly divisible by the generator. The receiver divides again, and any non-zero remainder means an error.

Which errors can CRC never miss?

All single-bit errors, all odd numbers of errors when the generator has x+1 as a factor, and every burst no longer than r bits. Longer bursts slip through with a probability of about 2⁻ʳ, which the benchmark confirms.

What is the difference between VRC and LRC?

VRC adds one parity bit to each character, a row check. LRC adds a whole parity character for the block, one bit per column. VRC misses even numbers of flips in one character, and LRC misses two flips in the same column.

How does the checksum work on both sides?

The sender adds the data as 16-bit words with end-around carry and sends the complement of the sum. The receiver adds every word including the checksum. A result of FFFF means no error was detected.

Why is Selective Repeat better than Go-Back-N on a bad link?

Go-Back-N discards every frame after a lost one and resends the whole window. Selective Repeat buffers out-of-order frames and resends only the missing ones. It needs more receiver memory and individual ACKs in exchange.

What limits the window size?

With m-bit sequence numbers, Go-Back-N can use a window of at most 2ᵐ − 1 and Selective Repeat at most 2ᵐ⁻¹. Larger windows let an old frame be mistaken for a new one with the same number.

Why use a random seed?

The channel noise comes from a pseudo-random generator. Fixing the seed makes every run repeatable, so a demo shows the same losses each time and results can be checked by someone else.
